# 9224 · Timbreloom

**The one question:** What if Resonance could re-voice your live singing/playing
into another instrument in real time — a DDSP-style additive resynthesizer you
hear *and* see?

Timbreloom is a real-time **harmonic-plus-noise resynthesizer** built entirely
from Web Audio DSP — **no neural network**. It follows the *decoder* half of the
DDSP model (Engel et al., ICLR 2020): a couple of interpretable control signals
(pitch + loudness) are turned back into sound by an additive harmonic bank plus a
filtered-noise band. Your **melody and dynamics survive; your timbre is
replaced** by the chosen instrument voice.

## How the DDSP decoder works here

1. **Live control extraction (the "encoder").** Each frame reads a 2048-sample
   time-domain buffer from the mic and computes:
   - **Fundamental pitch f0** via a YIN-lite normalized autocorrelation over the
     70–1000 Hz range, with parabolic peak interpolation and a voicing-confidence
     gate (`detectPitch`).
   - **Loudness** as an RMS envelope, smoothed with a fast-attack / slow-release
     one-pole filter.

2. **Harmonic-plus-noise decoder (the "synth").**
   - **Harmonic bank:** up to 64 sine partials at integer multiples of f0. Their
     relative amplitudes are a per-voice **spectral-envelope preset** (the
     "instrument"). For efficiency and automatic band-limiting these are baked
     into a single `PeriodicWave` driving one `OscillatorNode`; overall harmonic
     gain tracks loudness. Partials above Nyquist are dropped from the visual.
   - **Noise band:** a seeded white-noise buffer (deterministic `mulberry32`,
     never `Math.random`) through a `BiquadFilter` whose centre frequency and
     gain track loudness — the breath / bow / air of the harmonic-plus-noise
     model.
   - **Gate:** both the harmonic and noise gains gate on loudness, so **silence
     in → silence out**. There is no drone bed.

3. **Four voices** — `glass`, `reed`, `metal`, `wood` — each a different
   partial-amplitude envelope plus noise mix/filter. Switching a voice rebuilds
   the oscillator's periodic wave and the noise filter, so the same pitch and
   dynamics come out sounding like a different instrument.

## Visual (WebGPU primary, Canvas2D fallback)

The art is the live harmonic stack. Each frame the 64 partial amplitudes are
pushed as a new column into a scrolling `256 × 64` history buffer, uploaded to an
`r8unorm` texture, and rendered by a raw **WebGPU** fragment shader (no three.js)
as a glowing **ember spectrum** that drifts left over time — brightness/height is
each partial's current amplitude, with a comet-trail recency ramp, faint partial
gridlines, and vignette. Warm ember/amber on near-black.

**Graceful fallback:** if `navigator.gpu` is absent, the same history buffer is
drawn to a `<canvas>` 2D context via the identical ember palette, with a
`text-destructive` "WebGPU unavailable — showing fallback" note.

## Seeded self-demo

A deterministic pitch+loudness stream (a pentatonic phrase generated by
`mulberry32(0x9224)` with an ADSR-ish envelope and gentle vibrato) drives the
*full* mechanism the moment the page loads, so the visual animates within ~1s on
a muted phone with no mic. "Play a seeded demo" adds audio on a user gesture;
"Start mic" switches the control source to your live input.

## Honest limitations

- Monophonic only — the autocorrelation pitch tracker assumes a single voice and
  will wobble or octave-jump on polyphony, heavy vibrato, or noisy rooms.
- Pitch/loudness run at animation-frame rate (~60 Hz), not per audio sample, so
  fast transients are smoothed; this is an expressive resynthesizer, not a
  transparent codec.
- The spectral envelope per voice is a *static* preset scaled by loudness. Real
  DDSP predicts time-varying per-harmonic amplitudes from a trained network; here
  the timbre shape is fixed and only its level breathes with your dynamics.
- Latency is browser-dependent (getUserMedia + graph scheduling); this is a demo,
  not the sub-10ms target discussed in arXiv:2503.11562.
- Baking harmonics into a `PeriodicWave` means all partials share one loudness
  scalar; the per-partial motion you *see* is the (perceptually scaled) envelope,
  which matches what you hear but is not independently re-synthesized per partial.
